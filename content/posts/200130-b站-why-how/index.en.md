# Bilibili? Why & How?

Sometime in the second half of 2019, news about Bilibili (China's YouTube-like video community, originally built around anime, comics, and games) started appearing frequently in my information feeds.

-   "Dulijun", a Bilibili creator, steered several thousand followers to Cao Da
-   The rapid rise of Banfo Xianren and Wizard Economics on Bilibili
-   [Video Wars 2019](https://mp.weixin.qq.com/s/T5u1_oiVwuyW1qEuRS0RaQ)
-   Friends of mine who spend hours a day watching Bilibili videos

Although I had registered an account long before, I had always treated Bilibili as an ACG (anime, comics, and games) community and rarely opened it. When I finally took a proper look, I discovered how large its content pool had become — beyond animation there were tech, life, fashion, entertainment, advertising... "I genuinely learn things on Bilibili" is not an empty phrase.

![Bilibili's homepage categories](/migrated-images/markdown_20200129_2FcUWp7b13N8.png)

In December 2019 I tried to stake out a spot on Bilibili, hoping to reach 1,000 followers in 2020 (progress so far: 1%). It still feels rather difficult, because the content I'm currently making doesn't fit Bilibili's tone very well; the numbers look much better on Xigua Video (ByteDance's Chinese video platform). But it's early days — I'll keep at it for a year and then see how the numbers look.

The main reason for writing this post is to share some of my thinking and understanding about video content. It may well not be correct — it's a record.

This post has two parts: Why & How.

## Why make videos on Bilibili?

### 1 The video era has arrived, and Bilibili's barrier to entry is lower than Douyin's or Kuaishou's

Blog → Weibo (China's Twitter-like microblog) → Douyin (Chinese TikTok)/Kuaishou: media length went from long to short, and media form from text to images-plus-text to video. Behind this: as infrastructure gradually matures and the barrier to publishing gradually drops, content production and consumption slowly migrate from the top of society's pyramid to the entire pyramid.

Video is easier to understand and absorb than text-with-images, so naturally its audience is broader. Compare opening an article versus opening a video — the psychological weight of the two is not on the same level at all.

At the same time, faster and cheaper mobile networks plus short-video apps have trained users into the habit of watching video anytime, anywhere. The video era has arrived, full stop.

Some will say: short video only needs a phone to shoot, while long video may also need a computer for post-production — surely short video is the lower barrier? Fair point, the barrier to *shooting* is indeed lower for short video. But what I'm talking about is that **the barrier to building an account** is lower on Bilibili than on Douyin or Kuaishou.

The 15-second constraint of short video means every second must be carefully designed; fail to hook the viewer within a few seconds and the video gets swiped away. And the chaotic early land-grab era is long gone — posting a few casual videos no longer wins you a mass of followers. Behind nearly every account that has made it in short video today stands a professional team; topic selection, shooting, post-production, and promotion all demand serious effort. Unless a solo creator's content is far superior to everyone else's, scaling on Douyin or Kuaishou is hard.

Bilibili, although video production demands a certain threshold, also filters out part of the creator pool with that threshold. Meanwhile Bilibili's monetization for creators currently lags Douyin/Kuaishou: outside a handful of categories that can survive on sponsorships, plenty of content racks up view counts while the creator earns nothing (the incentive program is only an incentive). The result: competition on Bilibili is less fierce than on Douyin/Kuaishou.

Of course, creators not making money on Bilibili now doesn't mean they won't later — but by then, the difficulty of building an account will be another story entirely.

### 2 Bilibili's user demographics and stickiness — this world ultimately belongs to the young

According to Bilibili's 2019 Q3 financial report: 128 million monthly active users, 37.6 million daily active users, and 83 minutes of average daily viewing time per user. Users aged 18–35 account for 78% — young people are Bilibili's main force, and Bilibili's future.

![Bilibili's 2019 Q3 financial report](/migrated-images/markdown_20200129_G7BJqBG47qOR.png)

### 3 Bilibili is still in its growth phase and needs lots of long-tail content to expand its content pool

![Major YouTube content categories](/migrated-images/markdown_20200129_eb1V8Rn3rJpE.png)

Who making videos doesn't want to become the YouTube of China? Bilibili naturally does too. After a decade-plus of development, YouTube has formed a remarkably complete content ecosystem — like a video library, covering a vast range of long-tail content. That enormous content library plus efficient recommendation algorithms have made YouTube a solidly top-three global traffic pool.

Although Bilibili keeps breaking out of its original circle, its content pool still looks thin next to YouTube's; much long-tail content remains uncovered.

Long-tail content has a low ceiling, true, but 30% of 100K beats 1% of 1M by a wide margin. Being early is an advantage — moving in now to cover and claim certain long-tail niches beats wringing your hands later over a saturated, competitive ecosystem.

### 4 Bilibili's quality community atmosphere

Thanks to mechanisms like Bilibili's membership exam and its "little black room" (the penalty system that bans violators), the community's overall atmosphere is a class above other video platforms. On WeChat Official Accounts (the main publishing platform in China), Weibo, and other platforms, a creator who takes sponsorships gets no shortage of abuse in the comments — but on Bilibili, the bullet comments (danmaku, comments overlaid on the video) are all "congrats to the creator on landing a sponsor". Bilibili users are genuinely happy to see creators earn income from good content — an atmosphere that truly is precious in today's internet world.

## How?

### 1 Content is king — deep, high-quality content is the foundation of everything

This should be the most basic truth of the content industry: without sufficiently deep, quality content, every other tactic is a castle in the air that cannot last.

![A treasure of a creator: Wizard Finance](/migrated-images/markdown_20200129_tIrV7VO3q6Bg.png)

[Wizard Finance](https://space.bilibili.com/472747194), which rose in 2019: as of 2020-01-28, 12 videos, 2.09 million followers, nearly 20 million views.

[Banfo Xianren](https://space.bilibili.com/37663924): as of 2020-01-28, 10 videos, 500,000 followers, 10 million views.

Both have cultivated deep roots in their respective fields (finance and risk control), with high-quality content and extremely high barriers to entry. Though they've only made a handful of videos, individual videos clear a million views. Fan stickiness is extreme: even Wizard Finance's unformatted, bare text transcripts pull 40–50K reads per post on WeChat Official Accounts.

[Transcript: 2019–2020 Capital Markets Year in Review, a rundown of the year's major economic and financial events](https://mp.weixin.qq.com/s/NSyqWqhEVe2uqPwPeV1Buw)

When content is deep and good enough, follower quality, stickiness, and conversion all deliver outstanding numbers — and a sudden breakout becomes more likely one day.

### 2 How to choose your topic?

For a video creator, topic choice affects the later breadth of subject matter, production difficulty, ceiling height, monetization routes, and much more.

#### 2.1 YouTube, Copy to China

![Primitive Technology](/migrated-images/markdown_20200129_QYP1hBkCbRYf.png)

As the world's largest video content pool, YouTube lets you find all sorts of strange and wonderful channels. "Copy to China" runs here: when you stumble on a channel that interests you, check the video upload dates, view counts, and production difficulty, then see whether similarly positioned content is unclaimed back home in China.

If it's a blue ocean, congratulations — you've found an opportunity worth trying. Even if similar content already exists, you can still try to beat it with "they have it, I make it new".

#### 2.2 Upgrading text content from WeChat Official Accounts and other platforms into video

Video influencers will replay the rise of text-platform influencers; a lot of written content can be upgraded into video content. On [Newrank](https://www.newrank.cn/public/info/list.html?period=day&type=data) (a Chinese content-analytics platform), pick a category and study the topic selection and content of the big WeChat Official Account and Weibo creators — then see whether it can be told in video form.

![WeChat Official Account encyclopedia-category monthly chart](/migrated-images/markdown_20200129_xP6QBqQGj480.png)

#### 2.3 Draw on your own experience; grab niche domains

Many industries and experiences carry unique content moats — ordinary people or newcomers find it hard to produce deep, quality content there. If you have distinctive experience in some field that can be reused, digging deep in that field is entirely viable.

Take the CPA exam, for example — a rigid-demand qualification that is hard to pass: if you've passed it and have unique, **long-term shareable** experience, that's a vein to mine. Or advanced mathematics, which trips up plenty of students: the schools' recorded lectures online cover only a fraction of the vast demand. You can make small, focused explainer videos by question type and exam period, from the perspective of someone who's been through it, building your own distinctive content library.

![Searching CPA on Bilibili](/migrated-images/markdown_20200129_Fe0KIQtriKyM.png)

#### 2.4 How to monetize later

**Sales = traffic × conversion rate × average order value** — remember this formula.

Creator monetization comes down to a few routes: custom sponsorships, e-commerce, paid knowledge, and ad revenue share. Topic choice affects, at minimum, the breadth of monetization paths open to you.

**Custom sponsorships**: high per-deal value, but only top accounts in a few particular categories can live off them.

**E-commerce**: all three points in the formula need to be satisfied; it best fits beauty and fashion positioning. But if fan stickiness is high, custom merchandise revenue can be considerable — take *This Is China* by Xingqiu Yanjiusuo (a popular Chinese geography-media account): priced at ¥168 (about $24), it sold over 100,000 copies within 20 days of release.

![JD.com listing for This Is China](/migrated-images/markdown_20200129_emIJbactpVIH.png)

**Paid knowledge**: the same as e-commerce, except the goods become virtual knowledge and consulting — it tests fan stickiness hard. The creator needs real standing and influence in the field. For reference, look at the most active community hosts on Zhishi Xingqiu ("Knowledge Planet", a Chinese paid-community platform).

**Ad revenue share**: to be blunt, a few hundred thousand followers across Chinese platforms (Bilibili, Xigua, Baijiahao — Baidu's creator platform, Penguin Hao — Tencent's) can barely feed you on ad revenue share alone; only with substantial YouTube views does the exchange-rate gap make the returns reasonably decent. Living full-time off ad revenue is unlikely for most creators.

Of course, if money isn't a concern, just follow your interests. If you do intend to monetize, it's best to think positioning through early — pivoting is not easy.

### 3 Be time's friend; cultivate one field deeply

Honestly, video's early bonus period is over: on one side, competitors across the industry keep multiplying; on the other, platform rules keep maturing. This process will progressively wash out a large batch of low-quality, uncompetitive creators; the ones that endure will be the quality, deep content creators.

Making videos — from topic selection to production to promotion — is an energy-intensive business, and during cold start a solo creator faces a long stretch of few followers, few views, and no feedback. This is an elimination tournament; whether you can persist in that situation is a severe test of a creator's endurance.

But as long as you keep cultivating one field and **keep producing quality content, stretch the time scale**, and you'll carve out territory of your own in the end.

### 4 Understand algorithmic distribution to help your cold start

These days even fine wine fears a deep alley. In the early stages, creators should **make good use of the power of platform algorithms to promote themselves**.

![Collaborative filtering](/migrated-images/markdown_20200129_Kz1LaNx9KyA2.png)

Understanding how platform algorithms distribute content can meaningfully improve your cold-start efficiency. I recommend reading *Content Algorithms* (《内容算法》); here is the author's bio.

> **About the author**
>
> Yan Zehua, a contracted author on Jianshu (a Chinese writing platform). Product director of Zhihu's (China's Quora-like Q&A platform) knowledge market, responsible for paid-content product operations.
>
> Formerly a senior product manager at Jinri Toutiao (ByteDance's news aggregator), technical lead of "Uncle Kai Storytelling", and a search architect at Baidu. During his time at Toutiao he was successively in charge of data feeds and strategy distribution for Toutiao Video, fan monetization for Toutiao accounts, and strategy distribution for Weitoutiao, living through the rapid growth of both the video and fan businesses.

The basic principles of algorithmic distribution are similar across platforms, and Mr. Yan explains them clearly in plain language that ordinary people can understand. (You can get the book by replying 内容算法 — its Chinese title — in the message backend.)

A few brief strokes here: during creation and promotion, creators need to consider how to **make the machine read the content well** — can the title, cover, tags, and description give the machine an accurate positioning of the content?

An example: suppose a video is titled "Huge Fast & Furious scenes staged right in Beijing!", but the actual content is a street-racing video. The machine distributes it to viewers profiled as interested in "Fast & Furious"; the click-through and completion rates come back ugly, and the video ends up down-weighted instead.

Of course, the market also has plenty of behavior that maliciously skirts the edge of platform rules. For solo creators, my advice is to read the platform rules carefully and make content honestly. The lawless growth phase is over — maliciously exploiting the rules may pay off short term, but once the platform tightens its rules it will come back to settle accounts and punish the creators involved.

![How platforms score creator accounts](/migrated-images/markdown_20200129_2wMvj3OkQ39l.png)

Algorithmic distribution weighs many factors; a creator's credit score and past performance both affect how later work gets distributed. Drilling holes in the rules is, over the long run, a losing trade.
