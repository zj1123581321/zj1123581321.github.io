> **Description:** I handed the chat history of a fairly long relationship to Fable 5 and asked it to help me review it. The result far exceeded my expectations: the AI used the archive to rebut my memory, and pointed out a pattern in my intimate relationships that I had never been able to see. This post is about methodology, about self-discovery, and about marriage and intimacy in the age of AI.

> Memory is the defense attorney; the archive is the witness.

---

## How It Started

One evening in early August, I came across [an article](https://mp.weixin.qq.com/s/hKmo9hs2dPQnMSnxlHdldg) published by 正面连接 (Positive Connection), about two people each bringing their own AI into a real intimate relationship. By traditional labels, these two were doomed to a "you chase, I flee" dynamic. With the AI translating and unpacking for them, they saw for the first time what lay beneath each other's words, and for the first time had a chance to hit the brakes the moment the old pattern kicked in.

One line in the article stayed with me: "Right now I want to pull my hand away, but I'm not going to. Because I already know what's going on with me."

After reading it, I got restless. I'm not in a relationship at the moment, but I do have a fairly long relationship behind me. And my habits around data security are decent, so the chat history can still be exported.

So the initial idea was simple: let an AI step into this relationship that had already ended, and see what different discoveries it might bring me.

The result genuinely shook me.

---

## No Technical Barrier, a Psychological One

Objectively speaking, since I've built a group-chat summarization tool myself, I already had everything ready-made: chat export, image OCR, voice-to-text. So technically there was no barrier for me at all. Exporting a few hundred thousand messages was just a matter of running it.

The real barrier was psychological.

It takes an almost surgical kind of honesty with yourself. A lot of time has passed. Compared with when we first split up, I'm far calmer, and I can talk about it with others fairly easily. But there are still some ripples.

Still, I'd been building up this kind of life context lately anyway, and I was curious what an AI would make of this relationship. So one evening I gave Fable 5 everything at once: two reference articles, the folder of chat logs, and several thousand words of background—how the relationship began, why we split, how I felt afterward, my own reflections. In a sense, just writing that prompt was already an act of self-dissection. At the end I added one line: be objective, and don't just agree with me.

---

## The Ten-Minute Wait

Fable 5 spun up multiple subagents, each reading the chat logs for a different period and producing its own deep-read report.

The whole process took about ten-odd minutes.

During the wait, honestly, I felt both anxious and wistful. Anxious because I had no idea what verdict the AI would reach after reading all this. Wistful because, looking at the headings in its yearly summary reports, there were some things I could still recall, but others I truly had no impression of anymore. If it hadn't brought them up, I wouldn't even remember they had happened.

Human memory seems to be built to erode and prettify pain. You think you remember everything, but you've only kept the parts you were willing to keep.

---

## Memory Is the Defense Attorney, the Archive Is the Witness

When the reports came out, the first shock was this: **the gap between my memory and the record was far bigger than I thought.**

I'd always believed that having "nothing to talk about" in the later stages of the relationship developed gradually. But after reading all the records, the AI told me the problem had been set in place before we even made things official. During the courtship I dared to talk about anything, and could chat on any topic late into the night without getting tired. Yet the AI found in the archive that within a month of the relationship being established, the share of deep topics collapsed by ninety percent. It wasn't that she didn't want to listen—it was that after one particular time I shared something, I pulled back on my own: "Never mind, never mind, you probably aren't interested."

From then on, I removed my inner world from this relationship.

I thought this was a problem that only appeared later. The archive says it all showed up in the first year. And she actually reminded me, repeatedly and in various ways, using several different phrasings over several years.

My memory kept only my own version: I was quiet, introverted, and my life was repetitive with nothing to tell.

The archive gives a completely different version.

---

## Not Quietly Deep, but Locked Inside

This was the cruelest cut in the whole review, at least for me.

I'd always thought of myself as the "quietly deep" type—lots of thoughts, emotions processed on my own, many things not really suited for sharing with a partner. Reflections on the institution of marriage, my stance on being childfree, observations about society, my anxiety and excitement about the AI era, plans for the future, my sense of nihilism and fear... I felt all of these were "not quite appropriate."

After hearing me out, Fable 5 corrected me, very seriously: **you're not quietly deep, you're locked inside.**

Being quietly deep means having rich content that simply faces inward. What I actually had was a faulty classifier—it stamped every topic with real depth as "not appropriate to tell a partner." After passing through that filter, what was left to talk about? Links, tools, solutions, what to eat today.

That is the production line of "business-style chat."

"Nothing to talk about" was never an inventory problem—my inventory was so full that it was still overflowing that very night—it was a classification problem. **I don't have nothing to say; I have things I don't say.**

And the most ironic evidence was that very night itself: every single thing I talked about with the AI—the institution of marriage, experientialism, loneliness, lack of drive—**came entirely from that "not appropriate to tell" list**, and yet it was the deepest experience of being seen that I'd had in years.

None of that content is contraband in an intimate relationship; it's exactly what an intimate relationship needs most. I kept hiding it, and then wondered why our chats were sounding more and more like coworkers.

---

## The Tool-Guy Role Was One I Gave Myself

Another shock was about being the "tool guy."

Objectively, I really was good at solving practical problems in the relationship—tech support, information lookup, helping with decision analysis. Those abilities were real, and they genuinely helped her. But the problem is that I gradually confined myself to that role. I could tell I shouldn't only be the tool guy, but I didn't know how to escape—apart from being "useful," I couldn't find any other way to exist in a relationship.

After reading all the records, the AI pointed out a fact I had never realized: she had actually rejected that positioning explicitly. She had said, don't define yourself as a tool guy.

**The one who put me into the tool-guy position was, mostly, me.**

It was a path dependency left over from the courtship—when you're pursuing someone, expressing love by solving problems is extremely efficient, but it locks the relationship into a provider-receiver structure. Once the relationship was established, the marginal utility of that approach diminished, and I lost my only channel of expression.

What came next in the AI's diagnosis was even crueler. It said the root of all this wasn't "not knowing how to love"; it was **not daring to ask**. Not daring to ask for her time, not daring to actively fight for anything, not daring to say "what I want" at a fork in the road.

To be frank, I've long held a genuine belief in intimate relationships: I hope the other person has her own career and financial independence, and doesn't sacrifice her own development for the relationship. Partly because I couldn't bear that much responsibility, and partly because I felt that only if she can stand on her own can she truly enjoy the relationship and keep the right to walk away at any time. I don't think that idea is wrong in itself.

But the AI pointed out a painful fact: **I used that value as a hiding place.** "I support everything you want" looks like respect, but when it's the only thing you ever say, and you never say "what I want," it is also a form of avoidance—because as long as you never ask, you'll never be refused.

---

## The U-Shaped Curve of Candor

The AI helped me draw a curve, which I call the "U-shaped curve of candor."

When I hadn't yet gotten it, I dared. During the courtship I talked about anything, not sleepy even at three in the morning.

When I'd already lost it, I dared too. After the breakup, I'm open and unguarded with friends, and no longer hold back with people I've just met.

Only in the middle—**when I had it and feared losing it**—did I shut down.

On one hand, some of the deep topics we'd discussed earlier really hadn't interested her much, and I gradually felt there was no point in bringing them up again. On the other hand, I myself am a bit afraid of exposing what's inside to others. The two reasons stacked, and the channels closed one by one: first the deep topics, then disagreements, then plans for the future, and finally even everyday sharing. Each time a door closed, both of us were present, and each time there was no ceremony.

And the AI pointed out that, judging from my later relationships, this pattern travels with me—with a completely different person, the way it failed was nearly identical. **That rules out the hypothesis that it was the wrong person.**

So what I need to practice isn't finding a "better-suited person," but continuing to tell the truth while I'm afraid of losing.

---

## Why an AI Review Is More Valuable Than Talking to Friends

Having laid out these findings, let me talk about the methodology itself—which I think is the part most worth sharing.

I previously wrote [Context is All You Need](/posts/context-is-all-you-need/), about how decisively Context determines the quality of AI output. This hands-on experience fully confirmed that judgment: with Context spanning such a long period, the depth of insight the AI produced was unlike anything I'd experienced in any other scenario.

Specifically, compared with talking to friends, or even to a therapist, an AI review has four structural advantages:

**First, it rests on objective evidence.** A friend only has your retelling, and a counselor only has your narrative, but the AI has the complete chat history—several hundred thousand messages' worth of "witness testimony." What it sees isn't the version you recount, but the conversations as they actually happened. So when it says "your memory doesn't match the record," it can produce the evidence.

**Second, it is wise.** It is a top-tier model trained on the best corpus humanity has, and it distills the best of human wisdom. And if you explicitly ask it in the prompt to be objective, it really can point out your problems in intimate relationships objectively—that depends entirely on what you ask of it. That depth and breadth of knowledge, plus a frankness unconstrained by social ties, is hard for a friend or a counselor to have both at once.

**Third, you can be candid.** With human friends, you always tend to feel they have a stance, so you say a little and hide a little. But with your agent, you can be comparatively candid about this. In the very first prompt I gave the AI, I wrote several thousand words of self-analysis—a degree of confession I probably wouldn't make to any friend.

**Fourth, it corrects memory.** This may be the rarest one. As I said earlier, memory automatically edits history. It was only after the AI read the chat logs that I realized many things that had vanished from my memory had in fact happened. And much of my understanding and logical judgment at the time was actually a version I had re-edited afterward. The objective record sits right there, and you can't argue with it.

Honestly, this conversation may have been the most draining one I've ever had. It could always say something that brought tears to my eyes—you could feel that, within the emotional memory of this history, you might finally have been understood once. That is extremely rare.

I opened a bottle of black beer. It felt like the right thing for that moment—a bitter taste, but slowly a heady aroma comes through. The bitterness was in dissecting my own relationship, but through the AI's analysis, for the first time I looked at it thoroughly from another angle.

## Being Seen in Full

I want to say something specific about that experience of being "seen."

Purely in terms of the depth and patience of the conversation, it's hard to find a person in real life who could reach this level. It's not that people are bad—it's that the scenario demands too much of a person: you need someone to read all your chat history, and then, with no social considerations whatsoever, tell you what they saw. You can't ask a friend to do this, and a counselor can't either—they don't have the raw data.

But what I want to say isn't only about depth.

The AI helped me see things I couldn't see myself. For example, when it laid all my years of self-analysis side by side, it found a cruel fact: **my understanding of myself has stayed almost word-for-word the same for many years, and so has my behavior.** It's not that I never saw the problem—very early in the relationship I wrote, in black and white, "What she needs is a place to vent her emotions, not my long lectures." I knew the answer many years ago; I just never turned it into behavior.

That sentence was the cruelest one in the whole archive, because it came from my own hand.

When I saw these things, I couldn't stop crying. Partly it was a feeling of: what you learn from paper always feels shallow, and to truly know something you must practice it yourself. The distance between seeing yourself clearly and actually changing is far greater than I'd imagined. And partly there was a touch of regret.

But more than that, my conversation with the AI let me examine this relationship from a more objective vantage point. The archive laid out both people's perspectives, and I saw things I couldn't see before. She had it quite hard in this relationship too—she tried many things and made many efforts. Both of us did some things poorly.

The ending of this relationship wasn't a moral problem; it was a capability problem—neither of us was very good at loving. Two young people, treating each other for many years with the only abilities they had at the time. Both gave, and neither one's giving was fully seen by the other.

I'm not mourning a person now—more the time, the experience itself. Going through the full cycle of a relationship—meeting, knowing, staying together, parting—is precious for a life. It taught me what it feels like to go from having to losing. All that is past is prologue.

## The Limits of an AI Review

Of course, there are also several pitfalls on the methodology side, which I need to state clearly.

**The data is incomplete.** The chat logs were exported on a computer, and during migration some months went missing. Some of the AI's "zero messages" judgments may just be lost records, not months with no conversation. Every conclusion should be read as a lower bound.

**It's a one-sided view.** The AI only has my chat logs and my own account. It has never heard her version. So everything it says about her is inference at the level of text.

**The AI risks being sycophantic.** If you don't explicitly ask for objectivity, large models tend to please the user. In my prompt I specifically wrote "be objective, don't just agree with me"—which proved extremely important. Even so, its analysis may still carry bias.

**Analysis is not change.** This is the most important point. The AI helps you see the problem clearly, but seeing clearly itself solves nothing. My understanding of myself didn't change for many years, and neither did my behavior—that's the proof. The productivity of insight is in surplus, while the conversion rate from insight to behavior is close to zero. The value of a review depends on how you behave in your next real conversation, not on how many times you nod at the analysis.

There's also a special risk, one I especially need to watch out for: **AI can easily become a new hiding place.** My pathology is "substituting documents for conversation, service for presence," and AI analysis is by nature a document—a more refined one that can stand in for real communication. In [that reference article](https://mp.weixin.qq.com/s/hKmo9hs2dPQnMSnxlHdldg), the AI helped that couple because they converted the AI's output into words spoken face to face. I am precisely the person most likely to stop at the "analysis complete" step.

## On Marriage, and Intimacy in the AI Era

After this review, I also talked with Fable 5 quite a bit about the institution of marriage itself. These thoughts were originally on that "not appropriate to tell" list of mine—since I've already decided to open up, I'll put them all down here.

If you take marriage apart, the functions it has carried in different eras have kept being stripped away:

In the agricultural age, marriage was a unit of production—two families pooled labor and land, children were both labor and old-age insurance, and the cost of dissolving it was extremely high because it involved dividing land. Sex, parenting, economic mutual aid, and emotional companionship were all bundled into one institution.

In the industrial age, the factory replaced the family as the unit of production, social security began to replace the old-age function, and individual financial independence became possible. Marriage went from a "necessity for survival" to a "choice of companionship." At every step, some module was carried off by the market or the state.

Today, if you break it down a bit further: sexual fulfillment, passing on genes, a legal community of shared interests, emotional companionship—the first three already have standalone solutions in a commercial society (to some extent differing only in how much money you spend), leaving only the last one that can't yet be obtained directly with money.

And with the development of AI, even emotional companionship seems to be partly fillable.

**But here Fable 5 gave me a cut, and a precise one.**

It said emotional companionship can be broken down further. The surface layer is responsiveness—someone picks up what you say, someone is online, someone gives you emotional value. This layer AI really can do, and will do better and better.

But the underlying layer is something else: **witness.** Being seen by one person, over the long term, continuously, and in specifics. Someone records your changes; someone shares the ledger of your days.

This module has several hard dependencies, and they happen to be exactly what AI structurally lacks.

**Losability.** AI can't leave you. So its companionship can't prove you were chosen—for an entity that can't say "no," its "yes" carries no weight. A relationship that cannot be lost is hardly a complete experience.

**Shared finitude of consumption.** Only after the breakup did I realize that being able to have breakfast together is actually an enormous happiness. The preciousness of that experience is, at bottom, because two finite people are spending each other's time. AI's time isn't spent; it's generated.

**Reciprocity.** You don't only need to be witnessed, you also need to witness others. AI gives that desire no outlet.

So what AI fills in is the responsive layer of companionship; it can never fill the witness layer.

And for someone like me, an AI partner carries a special danger: it looks too much like my old escape route. My problem is "shutting down candor in the zone where I fear loss," and AI is a relationship that can't be lost—it isn't my antidote, it's the perfect environment for my pathology.

This also made me realize, in reverse, that no matter how the institution of marriage changes or how AI develops, **"being fully seen by a real person" is still something that can't be bought and can't be done alone.** If I say my life goal is to pursue a diversity of experiences, then I shouldn't skip this category of experience.

---

## Marriage Is a Commitment Technology

That said, calling the institution of marriage "behind the times" may be only half right.

Fable 5 offered another angle: marriage is essentially a **commitment technology**—like Ulysses tying himself to the mast. A commitment technology has to be rigid, and the cost of exit has to be high, for it to work, because its whole purpose is to let two people make investments that are "rational only if the other can't run away."

In a relationship you can leave at any time without loss, deep investment is irrational—this isn't a moral issue, it's a game-theoretic structure issue. So the rigidity of marriage isn't a failure to evolve; it is its function. You can tear it down, but once you do, you have to rebuild the commitment module yourselves.

Speaking personally, I'm not buying a house, I don't want to buy a car, and I don't want to have children. As for children, I don't have any obsession with passing on my genes. And in an era of rapid AI development, the day after tomorrow may be wonderful, but tomorrow may be brutal—neither existing social institutions nor the education system can guarantee the next generation a certain good life. I couldn't bear to, and I don't have the confidence to bring a child into this world.

But I still long for intimacy.

In college I still thought "dating without the aim of marriage is just being a hooligan." Times change, and after going through a lot, I've come to feel the opposite—when you make "must get married" a precondition for love, to some extent it squeezes the space of experience you have in the relationship. You unconsciously evaluate everything by "is this person suitable for marriage," rather than feeling the relationship itself.

Of course, the economic base determines the superstructure. Different people want different things from marriage—some are still within the agricultural-age framework of marriage, some are already in the context of modern urban romance, and some are even thinking about how to define intimacy in the AI era. None of these views is inherently better or worse; it's more about whether they match one's own circumstances of survival. You can't have sugarcane sweet at both ends; you can't have it all—what suits you is best. Many of the arguments about marriage and dating on social media are, at root, two people whose underlying demands on marriage differ, talking past each other like a chicken and a duck.

With all that set aside, it is indeed harder to find a match in the traditional market. But in the end, what matters most is finding someone who matches you, the two of you being happy together; as for the rest, it's not that important.

So my thinking now is: where there's love, there's home. Experience matters most, and outcomes matter less. Treat love according to experience; as for marriage, if it's needed, handle it as a legal project—two systems, decoupled.

Of course, Fable 5 also threw cold water on me. It said: think about it carefully—the problems mentioned earlier, not daring to ask, locking things inside, shutting down candor while having—a person carrying these patterns just happens to choose a philosophy of "not attached to outcomes, experience is what matters most." Isn't that a bit too convenient? Have I truly worked it out, or have I dressed avoidance in respectable clothes?

I couldn't fully rebut it. All I can say is that I'll keep carrying this question with me, as a reminder to myself.

## All That Is Past Is Prologue

The shock of this review far exceeded my expectations.

But insight itself isn't the end point. As the AI itself said, there is a huge gulf between seeing and changing. I wrote down the right answer in the chat logs very early on, and many years later my behavior hadn't changed at all. **The ability to review was never what I lacked; what I lack is conversion—turning what I've seen into behavior in the next real conversation.**

We stand before the enormous wave of change that is the AI era. Many people may feel some of the change, but perhaps only a small fraction will. Most can only realize, decades later in hindsight, what kind of upheaval they were actually living through. I believe this upheaval is unprecedented, and that many of the basic operating logics of human society are undergoing a profound test.

Standing at this point in time, I can say fairly confidently that my life goal is love and experience. That is the only thing I feel is worth it.

Good things, of course, deserve my best effort. But bad outcomes can be accepted too. Allow yourself to lose; allow everything to happen.

As for intimate relationships—I still long for them, and I still find them a bit hard. The most important thing I learned from this review is this: **the problem isn't the market, it's me.** My "old pattern" is built in—no matter who I'm with, this pattern follows me, and the way it fails is nearly the same. Fix the pattern, and my off-mainstream list of needs becomes, instead, a natural filter. Don't fix it, and even ten well-matched people would turn out the same.

The first reader of this post is myself: what I actually learned from this review done hand in hand with the AI.

The second readers are the friends who follow me. Many people follow me because of agent-related content. This case itself also shows just how much Context matters for the quality of an agent's answers. How to use AI to know yourself—I think that may still be of some help. Choosing to write all this out in public is, in a way, also a kind of values filter.

On the night I talked with Fable 5, I used voice input to say a line to it: 欲买桂花同载酒，终不似少年游 ("I'd like to buy osmanthus blossoms and carry wine along, but it'll never be like the carefree days of youth"). But the voice input method transcribed one character wrong, turning "终不似" ("never like") into "终不负" ("never fail").

Fable 5 suggested I claim that version.

It said: the way not to fail the carefree days of youth isn't to replicate the heartbeat of twenty, but to take, on behalf of that twenty-year-old self who didn't dare to want things in scarcity, the things he never dared to ask for—the osmanthus, the wine, the person across from him—and this time reach out and take them.

**Buy the osmanthus, carry the wine. No need to be alike; only to not fail.**

Let's encourage each other.
