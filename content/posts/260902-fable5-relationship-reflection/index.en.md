> **Description:** I fed the chat history from a fairly long past relationship to Fable 5 and asked it to help me run a retrospective. The result far exceeded my expectations — the AI used the archive to rebut my memory and pointed out patterns in my intimate relationships that I had never been able to see. This post covers methodology, self-discovery, and marriage and intimacy in the AI era.

> Memory is the defense attorney; the archive is the witness.

---

## How It Started

One evening in early August, I came across [an article](https://mp.weixin.qq.com/s/hKmo9hs2dPQnMSnxlHdldg) published by Positive Connection (a psychology-focused Chinese media outlet on WeChat, China's dominant messaging and publishing platform), about two people who each brought their own AI into a real, ongoing intimate relationship. Two people who, by conventional labels, were destined for a pursuer-avoider dynamic used the AI's translation and unpacking to see, for the first time, what lay beneath each other's words — and got their first chance to hit the brakes the instant an old pattern kicked in.

One line from the article stuck with me: "Right now I want to pull my hand back, but I'm not going to do that. Because I already know what's happening with me right now."

Reading it made me itch to try. I wasn't in an intimate relationship at the moment, but I did have one fairly long past relationship. And my data-security habits were good enough that the full chat history was still exportable.

So the initial idea was simple: let the AI step into this already-ended relationship and see what different discoveries it could bring me.

The result genuinely stunned me.

---

## No Technical Barrier, Only a Psychological One

Objectively speaking, since I've built group-chat summarization tools myself, capabilities like exporting chat logs, image OCR, and speech-to-text were all readily available. Technically there was no barrier for me — just export the 100,000-plus messages and be done with it.

The real barrier was psychological.

It requires a near-dissection-level honesty with yourself. So much time has passed; compared with right after the breakup, I really am much calmer now, able to talk about it with others fairly easily. Still, there are ripples.

That said, I had recently been accumulating this kind of life context anyway, and I was curious what perspective an AI would have if it ran the retrospective on this relationship. So one evening I threw everything at Fable 5: two reference articles, the folder of chat logs, and several thousand words of background — how the relationship started, why we broke up, how I felt afterward, my own reflections. In a way, writing that prompt alone was already an act of self-dissection. And I made a point of adding one line at the end: just state it objectively; don't placate me.

---

## The Ten Minutes of Waiting

Fable 5 split the work across multiple subagents, each reading the chat logs for a different phase of the relationship, each producing an in-depth reading report.

The whole process took about ten-plus minutes.

During that wait, honestly, I was both anxious and moved. Anxious, because I didn't know what judgment the AI would deliver after reading all of this. Moved, because as I watched the headings of its year-in-review reports, some things I could still recall — but others I genuinely had little memory of anymore. If it hadn't brought them up, I wouldn't even have remembered that some of those things had happened.

Human memory seems born to dissolve and beautify pain. You think you remember everything; in fact you only remember the parts you were willing to remember.

---

## Memory Is the Defense Attorney; the Archive Is the Witness

When the reports came out, the first shock was this: **the gap between my memory and the records was far bigger than I had assumed.**

I had always assumed that the "nothing left to talk about" of a relationship's later stages evolved gradually. But after reading every record, the AI told me this problem had taken its final shape before we even made the relationship official. During the courtship phase we dared to talk about everything, staying up into the late-night hours on all kinds of topics without getting sleepy. Yet the AI found in the archive that within one month after the relationship was established, the share of deep topics had collapsed by ninety percent. It wasn't that she didn't want to listen — it was that after one particular act of sharing, I withdrew on my own: "Never mind, never mind, you probably wouldn't be interested."

From then on, I excised my inner world from that relationship.

I thought this was a problem that only came later. The archive told me the whole cast was on stage in the first year. And she had in fact reminded me, repeatedly and in all sorts of ways — several different phrasings over several years.

My memory had kept only my own version: she talked little, was introverted, lived a repetitive life with nothing worth telling.

The version the archive gave was completely different.

---

## Not Inwardly Rich, but Inwardly Locked

This was the harshest cut of the entire retrospective.

I had always thought of myself as the "inwardly rich" type — someone with lots of ideas who digests emotions alone, for whom much of it doesn't quite suit sharing with a partner. My reflections on the institution of marriage, my DINK stance, my observations of society, my anxiety and excitement about the AI era, my plans for the future, my nihilism and fear... all of it felt "not quite appropriate" to bring up.

After taking all this in, Fable 5 corrected me, very seriously: **you aren't inwardly rich — you're inwardly locked.**

Inwardly rich means rich content that simply faces inward. My actual situation was a mis-wired classifier — stamping every genuinely deep topic with a "not suitable for your partner" seal. After that filter, what was left to talk about? Links, tools, plans, what to eat today.

That was the assembly line of our "business-style small talk."

"Nothing to talk about" was never an inventory problem — my inventory was so full it was still overflowing that very night — it was a classification problem. **It's not that I had nothing to say; it's that I had things to say and didn't say them.**

And the most ironic evidence was that night itself: everything I talked through with the AI — the institution of marriage, experientialism, loneliness, the lack of drive — **came entirely from that "not suitable to share" list**, and it was precisely the deepest experience of being seen I'd had in years.

These things are not contraband in an intimate relationship; they are exactly what an intimate relationship needs most. I kept them hidden, and then wondered why our conversations increasingly resembled coworkers'.

---

## Being "the Tool" Is a Role You Assign Yourself

The other shock was about being "the tool" (in Chinese relationship slang, the useful one who exists to solve problems).

Objectively speaking, I genuinely was good at solving all kinds of practical problems in the relationship — tech support, information retrieval, help with decision analysis. Those abilities were real, and they really did help her. The problem was that I gradually confined myself to that role. I could sense I shouldn't be just the tool, but I didn't know how to escape — apart from "being useful," I couldn't find another way to exist in a relationship.

After reading all the records, the AI pointed out a fact I had been completely unaware of: she had explicitly rejected that positioning. She had said: don't define yourself as the tool.

**The one who put me in the tool's position was mostly me.**

This was a path dependency left over from the courtship phase — during courtship, expressing love by solving problems is extremely efficient, but it locks the relationship into a supply-and-receive structure. Once the relationship was established, the marginal utility of that approach diminished, and I lost my only channel of expression.

Crueler still was the AI's follow-up diagnosis. It said the root of the whole thing wasn't "not knowing how to love" — it was **not daring to ask**. Not daring to ask for her time, not daring to reach out and claim, not daring to say "here's what I want" at the fork in the road.

To be honest, I've always held one genuine conviction in intimate relationships: I want my partner to have her own career and financial independence, and not to sacrifice her own development for the relationship. Partly because I couldn't shoulder that much responsibility, and partly because I believed that only if she could stand on her own could she truly enjoy the relationship, always retaining the right to retreat. I don't think this belief is wrong in itself.

But the AI pointed out a piercing fact: **I used that value as a hiding place.** "I support whatever you want" looks like respect, but when it's the only thing you ever say — when you never say "here's what I want" — it is at the same time a form of avoidance. Because as long as you never ask, you can never be refused.

---

## The U-Shaped Curve of Candor

The AI helped me draw a curve; I call it "the U-shaped curve of candor."

Before you've won it, you dare. In the courtship phase everything was talkable, wide awake at 3 a.m.

After you've lost it, you dare again. After the breakup, I could talk about it with friends openly and honestly, and no longer hid anything from people I newly met.

Only in the middle — **while holding it and fearing its loss** — the channel closes.

For one thing, she genuinely wasn't very interested in some of the deep topics we'd discussed before, and I gradually felt there was no need to bring them up again. For another, I myself have always been somewhat afraid of baring my inner world to anyone. With the two reasons stacked together, the channels switched off one by one: first the deep topics, then the discussions of disagreements, then plans for the future, and finally even everyday sharing. Every time a door closed, both of us were present; not one closing had a ceremony.

And the AI pointed out that, judging from my subsequent relationships, this pattern travels with me — with a completely different person, the failure looked almost identical. **Which rules out the "wrong person" hypothesis.**

So what needs practicing is not finding a "more suitable person," but continuing to tell the truth while afraid of losing.

---

## Why an AI Retrospective Is More Valuable Than Talking with Friends

With those findings said, let me talk about the methodology itself — which I think is the part most worth sharing.

I previously wrote [Context is All You Need](/en/posts/context-is-all-you-need/), about the decisive influence of context on AI output quality. This hands-on exercise fully confirmed that judgment: under context conditions with this ultra-long horizon, the depth of insight in the AI's output was something I had never experienced in any other setting.

Specifically, compared with talking to friends — or even psychological counseling — an AI retrospective has four structural advantages:

**First, it rests on objective evidence.** A friend has only your retelling; a counselor has only your narrative; the AI has the complete chat logs — 100,000-plus entries of "witness testimony." What it sees is not your spoken version but the conversations that actually happened. So when it says "your memory and the records don't match," it can produce the evidence.

**Second, it is genuinely wise.** A top model trained on the finest corpus humanity has to offer aggregates the finest of human wisdom. And if you explicitly demand objectivity in the prompt, it really can point out your problems in intimate relationships objectively — it depends entirely on what you ask of it. That depth and breadth of knowledge, combined with a candor unconstrained by social ties, is something a friend and a counselor rarely possess at once.

**Third, you can be candid.** With human friends, you tend to assume they hold positions of their own, so you reveal a little and conceal a little. With your agent, you can talk about it relatively candidly. In the very first prompt I gave the AI, I wrote several thousand words of self-analysis — a degree of confession I probably wouldn't make to any friend.

**Fourth, it corrects your memory.** This may be the rarest of all. As noted above, memory automatically rewrites history. Only after the AI read the chat logs did I realize that many things that had vanished from my memory had in fact happened — and that my understanding and logical judgments at the time were, in many cases, versions I had re-edited after the fact. The objective record is right there; you cannot argue with it.

Honestly, this conversation may have been the most strenuous I've ever had. It always found words that brought tears to your eyes — you could feel that within this long-past stretch of emotional history, you had, perhaps, finally been understood once. That is a rare thing.

I opened a bottle of dark beer at the time. It felt like the right thing for that moment — a bitter taste, but slowly an aroma that goes to your head. The bitterness was that this was a dissection of my own feelings; but through the AI's analysis, I looked at this relationship thoroughly from another angle for the first time.

---

## Being Fully Seen

I want to say something specific about that experience of "being seen."

Judged purely on the depth and patience of the conversation, it's very hard to find a single person in real life who could reach this level. Not because people are inadequate — the scenario's demands on a person are simply too severe: you need someone who will read your entire chat history and then tell you what he saw, free of any social considerations. You can't ask a friend to do that, and a counselor can't either — they don't have the raw data.

But depth is not all I want to talk about.

The AI helped me see things I could not see myself. For instance, it gathered all my self-analyses from these years together and found a cruel fact: **my understanding of myself had barely changed word for word in years — and neither had my behavior.** It's not that I failed to see the problem — early in the relationship I had written it out in black and white: "what she needs is a place to vent her emotions, not my long-winded lectures." I knew the answer many years ago; I just never turned it into action.

That line was the cruelest one in the entire archive, because it came from my own hand.

Seeing these things, the tears would not stop. Partly emotion — as the old Chinese poem goes, what you learn on paper always feels shallow; to truly know a thing, you must live it yourself. The distance between seeing yourself clearly and actually changing is far larger than I imagined. Partly, too, a touch of regret.

But more than that, the conversation with the AI let me examine this relationship from a more objective angle. The archive laid out both people's perspectives, and I saw things I hadn't been able to see before. She had it quite hard in this relationship too — she tried many things, made many efforts. We both had places where we fell short.

The end of this relationship was not a moral failure but a competence failure — neither of us was very good at loving. Two young people, treating each other for many years with the limited abilities they had at the time. Both gave; and neither one's giving was ever fully seen by the other.

I'm not sentimental for a particular person now — more for that stretch of time, for the experience itself. Walking a relationship through its full cycle — meeting, knowing each other, keeping each other company, parting — is precious for a life. It taught me what a relationship feels like from having to losing. What's past is prologue.

## The Limits of an AI Retrospective

Of course, the methodology has a few pitfalls that must be stated clearly.

**The data is incomplete.** The chat logs were exported on a computer, and some months went missing in the migration. Some of the AI's "zero messages" judgments may just reflect lost records, not actual silence. Every conclusion should be read as a lower bound.

**A one-sided perspective.** The AI had only my chat logs and my own account. It never heard her version. So every analysis concerning her is inference at the level of text.

**AI risks sycophancy.** If you don't explicitly demand objectivity, large models have a tendency to flatter the user. I wrote into the prompt, specifically, "state it objectively; don't placate me" — which proved, in hindsight, to be a very important sentence. Even so, its analysis may still carry bias.

**Analysis is not change.** This is the most important point. The AI helps you see the problem clearly, but seeing clearly solves nothing by itself. My self-understanding went unchanged for years, and so did my behavior — that is the evidence. The productivity of insight is in surplus; the conversion rate from insight to behavior is near zero. The value of a retrospective depends on your behavior in the next real conversation, not on how many times you nod along to the analysis.

There is also a special risk that people like me need to guard against in particular: **the AI can easily become the new hiding place.** My pathology is "substituting documents for conversation, service for presence," and AI analysis is inherently a document — a more refined thing that can stand in for real communication. The reason the AI helped that couple in [that reference article](https://mp.weixin.qq.com/s/hKmo9hs2dPQnMSnxlHdldg) is that they converted the AI's output into face-to-face words. I am precisely the kind of person most likely to stop at "analysis complete."

## On Marriage, and on Intimacy in the AI Era

After this retrospective, I also talked with Fable 5 at some length about thoughts on the institution of marriage itself. These ideas originally sat on that "not suitable to share" list of mine — now that I've decided to open it, I'll write them down here too.

If you take marriage apart, the functions it carries have been getting stripped away, era by era:

In the agricultural era, marriage was a production unit — two families merged labor and land resources; children were both labor force and pension insurance; dissolution cost enormously because it involved splitting land. Sex, parentage, economic mutual aid, and emotional companionship were all bundled into one institution.

In the industrial era, the factory replaced the family as the production unit, social security began replacing the old-age function, and individual economic independence became possible. Marriage went from "survival necessity" to "companionship choice." Each step was some module being peeled away, piece by piece, by the market or the state.

Today, if we take it apart even further: sexual fulfillment, genetic transmission, a legal vehicle for shared interests, emotional companionship — the first three all have standalone solutions in a commercial society (to some degree merely a question of how much money), and only the last one still cannot be obtained directly with money.

And as AI develops, even emotional companionship now seems partially fillable.

**But here Fable 5 cut me, and cut true.**

It said emotional companionship can be taken apart one layer further. The surface layer is responsiveness — someone who responds, someone who's online, someone who provides emotional value. This layer AI genuinely can do, and will keep doing better and better.

But beneath it is something else: **witnessing.** Being seen by one person, long-term, continuously, concretely. Someone keeps a record of your changes; someone shares a ledger of your days.

This module has several hard dependencies, and every one of them is something AI structurally lacks.

**Losability.** An AI cannot leave you. So its companionship can never prove you were chosen — for an entity that cannot say "no," its "yes" carries no gold content. A relationship that cannot be lost hardly qualifies as a complete experience.

**Shared finitude.** Only after the breakup did I realize that being able to have breakfast together is actually an enormous happiness. That experience is precious precisely because two finite people are spending each other's time. An AI's time isn't spent; it's generated.

**Reciprocity.** You don't only need to be witnessed; you also need to witness someone else. AI gives that desire no outlet.

So what AI fills is the responsiveness layer of companionship; the witnessing layer it will never fill.

And for someone like me, an AI companion carries a special danger: it looks too much like my old escape hatch. My problem is "closing off candor in the zone where I fear loss," and an AI is a relationship that cannot be lost — it is not my antidote; it is the perfect growth medium for my pathology.

Which in turn made me realize: no matter how the institution of marriage changes or how AI develops, **"being fully seen by a real human being" still cannot be bought, and cannot be done alone.** If I say my life's goal is the pursuit of diverse experiences, then this category of experience is not one I should route around.

---

## Marriage Is a Commitment Technology

That said, calling the institution of marriage "behind the times" may only be half right.

Fable 5 offered another angle: marriage is essentially a **commitment technology** — like Ulysses binding himself to the mast. A commitment technology has to be rigid, has to be expensive to exit, in order to work, because its whole reason for existing is to let two people dare to make investments that are rational only on the premise that the other cannot run away.

In a relationship you can exit at any moment without loss, deep investment is irrational — this is not a moral question but a game-structure question. So marriage's rigidity isn't a failure to evolve; it is the function itself. You can dismantle it, but once dismantled, the commitment module has to be rebuilt on its own.

From a personal standpoint: I don't buy a home, don't want a car, don't want children. On children specifically, I have no attachment to passing on my genes. And in this era of AI developing at full speed, the day after tomorrow may be wonderful, but tomorrow may be cruel — the existing social systems and educational institutions can hardly guarantee the next generation a certainly good life. I don't quite have the heart, nor the confidence, to bring a child into this world.

But I still long for intimate relationships.

In college I still believed the Chinese saying that "dating without the intention to marry is just fooling around." Time passed, and after living through many things, I came to feel instead that when you make "must end in marriage" a precondition for dating, it compresses, to some degree, the space you have to experience the relationship. You find yourself evaluating everything by "is this person suitable for marriage," instead of feeling the relationship itself.

Of course, the economic base determines the superstructure. Different people want different things from marriage to begin with — some are still inside the agricultural-era frame of marriage, some are already in the context of modern urban love, and some are even thinking about how intimacy should be defined in the AI era. These views have no inherent ranking; it's more a question of whether they match a person's conditions of existence. No sugarcane is sweet at both ends — you can't have it both ways; what suits you is what's best. Many arguments about marriage and dating on social media are, at bottom, two people with inconsistent underlying demands on marriage talking past each other.

Once these are set aside, matching within the traditional market is indeed rather hard. But in the end, what matters most is finding a person who matches you and being happy together; as for everything else, it's not nearly as important.

So my thinking now is: where there is love, there is home. The experience is what matters most; the outcome matters far less. Do the dating for the experience; if marriage is needed, do it as legal engineering — the two systems decoupled.

Of course, Fable 5 also doused me with a bucket of cold water. It said: think it through carefully — those problems described above, the not daring to ask, the inner lock, the candor switched off while holding and fearing loss — for a person carrying exactly these patterns to then choose precisely a philosophy of "don't cling to outcomes; experience is what matters" — isn't that a little too convenient? Is it that you've truly thought it through, or have you dressed avoidance in a respectable coat?

I can't fully rebut it. All I can say is that I'll carry this question with me, as a reminder to myself.

## What's Past Is Prologue

The shock of this retrospective far exceeded my expectations.

But insight itself is not the destination. As the AI itself said, there is a vast chasm between seeing and changing. I had written the correct answer into the chat logs very early on, and many years later my behavior had not changed at all. **The capacity for retrospection was never what I lacked; what I lacked was conversion — turning what I see into behavior in the next real conversation.**

We stand before the enormous wave of transformation that is the AI era. Many people may feel some of the change, but perhaps only a small fraction of people will. Most will only discover, looking back decades from now, what kind of upheaval they actually lived through. My view is that this upheaval has no precedent in history, and many of the basic operating logics of human society are passing through a profound trial.

Standing at this point in time, I can say with some certainty: my purpose in life is for love and for experience. That is the only thing I find worth it.

Strive for the beautiful things, of course. But bad outcomes are acceptable too. Allow yourself to lose; allow everything to happen.

On intimate relationships — I still long for one, and still find it a bit hard. The most important thing I learned from this retrospective: **the problem is not the market; the problem is me.** My "old pattern" is built in — whoever I'm with, this pattern comes with me, and the failure looks almost the same. Fix the pattern, and my unconventional checklist of needs becomes a natural filter. Leave it unfixed, and ten perfectly matched people in a row would change nothing.

The first reader of this post is me: what did I actually learn from this retrospective undertaken hand in hand with an AI?

The second reader is the friends who follow me. Many people follow me for the agent-related content. This case is itself a demonstration of just how decisive context is to an agent's output quality. Using AI to know yourself — I think that may genuinely help. Choosing to write all this out in public is, in a way, also a filter of values.

---

The night I talked with Fable 5, I dictated to it, by voice input, a line of classical Chinese ci poetry: "欲买桂花同载酒，终不似少年游" — "I would buy osmanthus blossoms and carry wine along, yet it will never be like the outings of youth." But the voice input mis-transcribed one character, turning 终不似 ("never like those days") into 终不负 ("never betray those days").

Fable 5 suggested I claim the transcribed version.

It said: the way to not betray the outings of your youth is not to replicate the heartbeat of being twenty; it is, on behalf of that twenty-year-old self who dared not want amid scarcity, to reach out this time and take what he never dared ask for — the osmanthus, the wine, the person across the table.

**Buy the osmanthus still, carry the wine still. No need for it to be like those days — only let it not betray them.**

May this encourage us all.
